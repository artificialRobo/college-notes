# 4.1.2 Prostheses

## 1. What is a prosthesis?

A **prosthesis** is an artificial device designed to **replace a missing body part or to restore part or all of its lost function**.

In simple terms, when a body part is absent because of congenital conditions, injury, disease, or surgical removal, a prosthesis can provide a substitute that performs some of the functions of the missing part.

Examples include:

* artificial limbs such as prosthetic legs and arms,
* artificial hands and feet,
* artificial eyes,
* and certain externally or internally used replacement devices.

A **prosthetic device** should not be confused with an **orthosis**. A prosthesis replaces a missing body part, whereas an orthosis supports, stabilizes, or corrects an existing body part.

---

## 2. Why are prostheses needed?

Loss of a body part can affect much more than physical appearance. It can interfere with:

* movement,
* balance,
* ability to perform daily activities,
* independence,
* occupational activities,
* and psychological well-being.

The primary purpose of a prosthesis is therefore **functional restoration**.

For example, loss of a lower limb can make standing and walking difficult. A properly designed prosthetic limb can restore a substantial degree of mobility by providing an artificial structure through which forces can be transmitted between the person and the ground.

The engineering challenge is not simply to make an artificial replacement that looks like the missing body part. It must interact safely and effectively with the human body.

---

## 3. Biomechanical principle of a prosthesis

A prosthesis functions as part of a combined **human–device mechanical system**.

Consider a lower-limb prosthesis:

```text
        Body
         │
         v
   Residual limb
         │
         v
   Prosthetic socket
         │
         v
   Prosthetic components
         │
         v
       Foot
         │
         v
      Ground
```

For movement to occur, forces must pass through this system.

The person generates muscular forces and controls body movement. These forces are transmitted through the residual limb and prosthesis to the ground. The ground simultaneously produces a **ground reaction force** on the prosthesis.

Thus, prosthetic design involves both:

* **force transmission**, and
* **motion control**.

The prosthesis must be sufficiently strong to withstand these forces while also allowing appropriate movement.

---

## 4. Important biomechanical requirements

A good prosthesis must satisfy several requirements simultaneously.

### 4.1 Mechanical strength

The prosthesis must withstand the forces generated during activities such as:

* standing,
* walking,
* climbing stairs,
* running,
* and, where appropriate, sporting activities.

Failure of a load-bearing prosthetic component can cause serious injury.

### 4.2 Appropriate stiffness

The prosthesis must have appropriate **stiffness**, meaning appropriate resistance to deformation.

If it is too flexible, excessive deformation may reduce stability and control.

If it is excessively stiff, it may transmit uncomfortable or abnormal forces to the residual limb.

Therefore, the objective is not simply **maximum stiffness**, but **appropriate mechanical behavior for the intended function**.

### 4.3 Low weight

Excessive mass makes a prosthesis harder to control and can increase the energy required for movement.

Modern prostheses therefore often use lightweight materials such as advanced polymers, composites, and selected metals.

### 4.4 Comfort

The device must interact comfortably with the remaining biological tissues.

A mechanically strong prosthesis can still be unsuccessful if it causes:

* excessive pressure,
* friction,
* skin irritation,
* pain,
* or tissue damage.

### 4.5 Stability

The prosthesis must help the user maintain appropriate balance and control.

This is particularly important for lower-limb prostheses because they must support body weight during standing and movement.

### 4.6 Functional compatibility

The prosthesis should reproduce as closely as reasonably possible the important mechanical functions of the missing body part.

For example, a prosthetic foot should provide appropriate support during stance and controlled movement during walking.

---

## 5. Main components of a prosthetic limb

The exact components depend on the type of prosthesis, but a lower-limb prosthesis commonly contains the following elements:

```text
       Residual limb
            │
            v
     ┌─────────────┐
     │   Socket    │
     └─────────────┘
            │
            v
   Suspension system
            │
            v
   Knee / structural
       components
            │
            v
     Ankle / foot
            │
            v
         Ground
```

### 5.1 Residual limb

The **residual limb** is the remaining portion of a limb after amputation.

It is biologically important because it provides the interface between the person's body and the prosthesis.

Its condition, shape, tissue characteristics, muscle strength, and sensitivity affect prosthetic fitting.

### 5.2 Socket

The **socket** is the component into which the residual limb fits.

It is one of the most important parts of a prosthetic limb because it provides the mechanical connection between the person and the artificial components.

The socket must distribute forces appropriately over the residual limb.

An improperly designed socket can concentrate pressure in particular areas and cause discomfort or tissue injury.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Good socket design} \rightarrow
\text{better force distribution} \rightarrow
\text{greater comfort and control}}
$$

### 5.3 Suspension system

The **suspension system** keeps the prosthesis securely attached to the body.

A prosthesis must not move excessively relative to the residual limb during walking or other activities.

Different suspension approaches may use mechanical attachment, suction, liners, or other systems.

The choice depends on factors such as the user's anatomy, activity level, skin condition, and type of prosthesis.

### 5.4 Structural components

Structural components connect the major parts of the prosthesis and transmit mechanical loads.

They must be:

* strong,
* sufficiently lightweight,
* durable,
* and appropriately stiff.

Materials used may include metals, polymers, and fiber-reinforced composites.

### 5.5 Joints

Some prostheses contain artificial joints corresponding to biological joints.

For example, a transfemoral prosthesis may include an **artificial knee**.

The knee component must control movement during different phases of walking and provide appropriate stability.

More advanced designs can incorporate mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, or electronic control mechanisms.

### 5.6 Prosthetic foot

The prosthetic foot supports the body and interacts with the ground.

Its design affects:

* balance,
* shock absorption,
* energy storage and return,
* and walking efficiency.

Some prosthetic feet use compliant structures that deform during loading and return some stored mechanical energy during movement.

---

## 6. Types of prostheses

Prostheses can be classified using several different criteria. There is no single classification that describes every prosthetic device.

A useful classification is based on the **body part replaced**.

### 6.1 Upper-limb prostheses

These replace parts of the upper extremity, such as the:

* hand,
* forearm,
* or arm.

Their design must address functions such as:

* grasping,
* holding,
* positioning,
* and manipulation of objects.

Upper-limb prostheses therefore involve both mechanical movement and, in advanced systems, control of artificial actuators.

### 6.2 Lower-limb prostheses

These replace parts of the lower extremity, such as the:

* foot,
* lower leg,
* or entire limb.

Their primary functions include:

* supporting body weight,
* maintaining stability,
* absorbing or returning mechanical energy,
* and enabling walking.

Because lower limbs repeatedly experience substantial loads, mechanical strength and fatigue resistance are particularly important.

---

## 7. Classification based on method of operation

Prostheses can also be classified according to how they generate or control movement.

### 7.1 Passive prostheses

A **passive prosthesis** does not actively generate movement using a powered actuator.

Its movement results mainly from the user's own forces and from the mechanical properties of the prosthetic components.

Examples include mechanically simple artificial limbs and certain prosthetic feet.

#### Advantages

* relatively simple construction,
* lower maintenance requirements,
* potentially lower cost,
* and no active power requirement for movement.

#### Limitation

The ability to reproduce complex biological movement is limited.

### 7.2 Body-powered prostheses

A **body-powered prosthesis** uses the user's own body movement to operate the device.

For example, movement of the shoulder or other body regions can be transferred through a mechanical cable system to operate an upper-limb prosthetic mechanism.

```text
User movement
      ↓
Mechanical cable/linkage
      ↓
Prosthetic mechanism
      ↓
Artificial movement
```

The major advantage is that the system can be relatively robust and does not necessarily require an external electrical power source.

However, operation may require physical effort and may provide less natural movement than advanced powered systems.

### 7.3 Externally powered prostheses

An **externally powered prosthesis** uses an external energy source, commonly electrical power, to operate actuators.

An **actuator** is a device that converts supplied energy into mechanical motion.

The general system is:

```text
Control signal
      ↓
Controller
      ↓
Actuator
      ↓
Mechanical movement
```

Externally powered systems can provide more active movement than purely passive systems.

### 7.4 Myoelectric prostheses

A **myoelectric prosthesis** uses electrical signals associated with muscle activity to control an artificial device.

**Myoelectric** means "related to electrical activity generated by muscles."

When a person attempts to contract a muscle, electrical activity can be detected using electrodes.

A simplified control sequence is:

```text
User attempts movement
        ↓
Muscle activation
        ↓
Electrical signal detected
        ↓
Signal processing
        ↓
Controller interprets signal
        ↓
Motor/actuator activated
        ↓
Prosthetic movement
```

This provides a direct relationship between the user's intended movement and the movement of the prosthesis.

#### Important limitation

Myoelectric control is not identical to natural biological control. Signals can vary among users and may be affected by electrode placement, muscle condition, noise, and other factors.

---

## 8. Advanced prostheses and control

Modern prosthetic systems may combine:

* sensors,
* electronic controllers,
* actuators,
* mechanical components,
* and software algorithms.

A simplified architecture is:

```text
       User
         │
         v
     Biological
       signals
         │
         v
      Sensors
         │
         v
 Signal processing
         │
         v
     Controller
         │
         v
     Actuator
         │
         v
 Prosthetic movement
         │
         v
 Mechanical interaction
         │
         └──────> feedback
```

The **feedback** component is important because the system can use information about movement, force, position, or loading to adjust its behavior.

This is one of the major differences between a simple mechanical replacement and an advanced **smart prosthetic system**.

---

## 9. Prosthesis–body interface

One of the central problems in prosthetic engineering is the **interface** between the artificial device and the biological body.

At this interface, mechanical loads are transferred between:

$$
\text{body} \leftrightarrow \text{prosthesis}
$$

The interface must achieve three objectives:

1. securely connect the prosthesis to the body,
2. transfer mechanical forces,
3. minimize harmful stresses on biological tissues.

This is particularly important for the socket of a limb prosthesis.

If the load is distributed poorly:

```text
Poor load distribution
        ↓
Localized high pressure
        ↓
Pain / tissue irritation
        ↓
Reduced prosthesis use
        ↓
Reduced functional benefit
```

Thus, **biomechanical compatibility is as important as mechanical strength**.

---

## 10. Materials used in prostheses

Material selection is an important engineering decision.

A prosthetic material should generally have an appropriate combination of:

* strength,
* stiffness,
* low density,
* durability,
* corrosion resistance,
* wear resistance,
* and biocompatibility where it contacts biological tissues.

Common material classes include:

### Metals

Examples include titanium alloys and certain stainless steels.

They are useful because of their strength and durability.

### Polymers

Polymers can provide low weight and flexibility and are used in various structural and interface components.

### Fiber-reinforced composites

Composite materials combine a matrix material with reinforcing fibers.

They can provide a favorable combination of:

* high strength,
* relatively low weight,
* and directional mechanical properties.

The selection depends on the specific component and its mechanical requirements.

---

## 11. Design requirements: balancing competing properties

Prosthetic design involves **trade-offs**.

For example:

```text
Higher strength
      ↕
Usually increased material requirement
      ↕
Potential increase in weight

Lower weight
      ↕
May require advanced materials/design
      ↕
Must still maintain adequate strength
```

Similarly:

* greater stiffness can improve structural stability,
* but excessive stiffness may increase undesirable loading;
* complex control can improve functionality,
* but it can also increase cost, maintenance, and system complexity.

Therefore, prosthetic engineering aims for an **optimal combination of properties**, rather than maximizing one property independently.

---

## 12. Advantages of prostheses

Well-designed prostheses can provide significant benefits:

* restoration of lost physical function,
* improved mobility,
* greater independence,
* improved ability to perform daily activities,
* improved participation in work and recreation,
* and improved body image in some users.

Advanced prostheses can provide increasingly sophisticated movement and control.

However, the effectiveness of a prosthesis depends not only on technology but also on **proper fitting, training, rehabilitation, and individual user requirements**.

---

## 13. Limitations and challenges

Despite major advances, prosthetic systems do not completely reproduce natural biological limbs.

Important challenges include:

### 13.1 Lack of natural sensory feedback

A natural limb provides continuous information about:

* pressure,
* position,
* movement,
* temperature,
* and other sensations.

Many prostheses cannot reproduce this sensory information fully.

### 13.2 Comfort and interface problems

Poor fitting can cause:

* pressure,
* friction,
* skin problems,
* and pain.

### 13.3 Control complexity

Human movement involves highly coordinated nervous-system control. Reproducing this with sensors, controllers, and actuators is difficult.

### 13.4 Energy requirements

Powered prostheses require an energy source, such as a battery. Energy capacity and weight can therefore become important design constraints.

### 13.5 Cost

Advanced components and control systems can be expensive.

### 13.6 Mechanical wear and fatigue

Prostheses experience repeated loading. Components can therefore undergo **fatigue**, meaning progressive damage caused by repeated or cyclic loading.

This makes durability and maintenance important.

---

## 14. Prosthesis versus natural limb

The goal of prosthetic engineering is generally **functional restoration**, not perfect duplication.

| Feature | Natural limb | Prosthetic limb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Force generation | Biological muscles | User, mechanical systems, and/or actuators |
| Control | Nervous system | Mechanical/electronic control and user input |
| Sensory feedback | Naturally integrated | Usually limited or artificially provided |
| Adaptation | Biological adaptation | Primarily engineered adaptation/control |
| Energy source | Metabolic energy | User energy and/or external power |
| Repair | Biological healing in many cases | Mechanical maintenance/replacement |

The natural limb is an integrated biological system with sensing, control, actuation, and adaptation. A prosthesis attempts to reproduce selected functions using engineering systems.

---

## 15. Example: biomechanical design of a prosthetic leg

Consider a person with a lower-limb amputation.

The prosthetic system must:

1. provide a secure connection to the residual limb,
2. support body weight,
3. transmit forces safely,
4. provide appropriate joint movement,
5. interact with the ground,
6. maintain stability during stance,
7. permit controlled movement during walking,
8. and withstand repeated loading.

During walking, the loading pattern changes continuously.

```text
Heel contact
     ↓
Loading of prosthetic foot
     ↓
Weight transfer
     ↓
Mid-stance
     ↓
Forward movement
     ↓
Push-off
     ↓
Swing phase
     ↓
Next contact
```

The prosthesis must therefore function under **dynamic and cyclic loading**, not simply under a single static force.

This illustrates why knowledge of biomechanics is essential to prosthetic design.

---

## 16. Key relationship between biomechanics and prosthetic engineering

The central idea can be summarized as:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Tissue mechanics}
+
\text{Human movement}
+
\text{Mechanical design}
\rightarrow
\text{Functional prosthesis}
}
$$

The mechanical properties of the residual tissues determine how forces can safely be transferred. Human biomechanics determines the required movements and loading patterns. Engineering design then determines the geometry, materials, joints, control systems, and interfaces needed to achieve the desired function.

Thus, prostheses are a direct application of biomechanics.
