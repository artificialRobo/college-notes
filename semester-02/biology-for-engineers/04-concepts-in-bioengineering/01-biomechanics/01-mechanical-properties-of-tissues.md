# 4.1.1 Mechanical Properties of Biological Tissues

## 1. What is biomechanics?

**Biomechanics** is the application of principles of **mechanics** to biological systems. Mechanics is the branch of physics concerned with how forces produce motion, deformation, or equilibrium in objects.

In bioengineering, biomechanics is mainly concerned with understanding how **biological tissues, organs, cells, and body structures respond to mechanical forces**.

A useful formal definition is:

> **Biomechanics is the study of the mechanical behavior of biological systems and the effects of forces, stresses, strains, and motion on living tissues and structures.**

In this topic, the main focus is the **mechanical properties of biological tissues**, which is, how tissues behave when an external force is applied to them.

Examples include:

* Bone resisting body weight and impact.
* Tendons transmitting forces from muscles to bones.
* Ligaments stabilizing joints.
* Cartilage allowing relatively smooth movement at joints.
* Skin stretching when the body moves.
* Blood vessels expanding and contracting as blood pressure changes.

Understanding these properties is essential for designing **prostheses, implants, artificial joints, rehabilitation devices, and other biomedical systems**.

---

## 2. Why are the mechanical properties of biological tissues important?

A biological tissue is not simply a passive material. Its mechanical behavior determines how effectively it performs its biological function.

For example, **bone** must be sufficiently strong and stiff to support the body. **Tendon** must withstand tensile forces while transmitting muscle forces to bone. **Cartilage** must tolerate repeated loading while providing a low-friction surface for joint movement.

Therefore, engineers need to know:

1. how much force a tissue can withstand,
2. how much it deforms under that force,
3. whether the deformation is temporary or permanent,
4. how it behaves under repeated loading,
5. and how its mechanical behavior changes with time, direction, age, injury, or disease.

This information is used when designing devices that interact mechanically with the body.

For instance, an artificial hip joint should have sufficient strength and wear resistance, but its mechanical behavior should also be compatible with the surrounding bone. A material that is excessively stiff compared with bone can alter the way mechanical loads are transferred through the skeleton.

## 3. Basic mechanical concepts required for biomechanics

Before discussing tissue properties, several mechanical terms must be understood.

### 3.1 Force

**Force** is an interaction that can change the motion or shape of an object.

It is measured in **newtons (N)**.

In biomechanics, forces can arise from:

* body weight,
* muscle contraction,
* external impact,
* gravity,
* contact with another object,
* or forces generated by medical devices.

For example, when a person stands, body weight produces forces that are transmitted through the bones of the lower limbs.

### 3.2 Stress

When an external force acts on a material, the material internally resists that force.

**Stress** is the internal resisting force acting per unit area.

$$
\sigma = \frac{F}{A}
$$

where:

* $\sigma$ = stress,
* $F$ = applied force,
* $A$ = cross-sectional area.

The SI unit of stress is **pascal (Pa)**, although biological and engineering materials are often described using kPa, MPa, or GPa.

#### Example

Suppose a tissue has a cross-sectional area of (10,mm^2) and is subjected to a force of (100,N).

$$
A=10,mm^2=10\times10^{-6},m^2
$$

Therefore,

$$
\sigma=\frac{100}{10\times10^{-6}}
=10^7,Pa
=10,MPa
$$

Thus, the applied stress is **10 MPa**.

#### Why stress matters

Using stress rather than force allows materials of different sizes to be compared. A force of 100 N produces a different mechanical effect on a large tissue section than on a very small one.

---

## 4. Strain

When a force is applied, a tissue may change its dimensions.

**Strain** is the relative deformation produced in a material.

For tensile deformation:

$$
\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L_0}
$$

where:

* $\varepsilon$ = strain,
* $\Delta L$ = change in length,
* $L_0$ = original length.

Strain is dimensionless because it is a ratio, although it is often expressed as a percentage.

### Example

If a tendon initially has a length of 100 mm and stretches by 2 mm:

$$
\varepsilon=\frac{2}{100}=0.02
$$

or

$$
\varepsilon=2%
$$

So the tendon experiences **2% strain**.

---

## 5. Types of mechanical loading

Biological tissues can experience different kinds of mechanical loading.

### 5.1 Tension

**Tension** occurs when forces pull a material apart.

Example: a tendon being pulled by a contracting muscle.

### 5.2 Compression

**Compression** occurs when forces push a material together.

Example: vertebrae experiencing compressive forces when supporting body weight.

### 5.3 Shear

**Shear** occurs when forces act parallel to a surface but in opposite directions.

Example: layers of tissue sliding relative to one another.

### 5.4 Bending

**Bending** occurs when a structure is subjected to forces that cause it to curve.

Bones can experience bending during activities such as walking or jumping.

### 5.5 Torsion

**Torsion** occurs when a twisting moment acts on a structure.

For example, bones can experience torsional loading when the body rotates while the foot remains relatively fixed.

In reality, biological tissues often experience **combined loading**, rather than a single type of loading.

## 6. Mechanical properties of biological tissues

The mechanical behavior of a tissue is described using several important properties.

The most important ones for examination purposes are:

* stiffness,
* strength,
* elasticity,
* plasticity,
* toughness,
* viscoelasticity,
* and anisotropy.

These properties are related but **not interchangeable**.

### 6.1 Elasticity

**Elasticity** is the ability of a material to return toward its original shape after the applied load is removed, provided the material has not been permanently damaged or deformed.

In simple terms:

**Load applied -> tissue deforms -> load removed -> tissue returns toward original state.**

For an ideal linear elastic material:

$$
\sigma = E\varepsilon
$$

where $E$ is **Young's modulus**.

Biological tissues are generally more complex than ideal elastic materials, but this relationship is useful for understanding their behavior over appropriate ranges.

### 6.2 Young's modulus and stiffness

**Young's modulus**, or **elastic modulus**, measures the resistance of a material to deformation under tensile or compressive loading.

$$
E=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}
$$

A larger value of $E$ generally means that greater stress is required to produce a given strain.

Therefore:

* **High modulus -> relatively stiff material**
* **Low modulus -> relatively compliant material**

For example, cortical bone is much stiffer than soft tissues such as skin.

### Important distinction

**Stiffness** and **strength** are not the same.

* **Stiffness** describes resistance to deformation.
* **Strength** describes the maximum stress a material can withstand before failure.

A material can be stiff but relatively brittle, or comparatively flexible but capable of sustaining substantial deformation before failure.

---

## 7. Stress–strain relationship

One of the most important ways of studying the mechanical behavior of a tissue is through a **stress–strain curve**.

A simplified representation is:

```text
Stress
  │
  │                    Failure
  │                       ●
  │                     /
  │                   /
  │                __/
  │             __/
  │          __/
  │       __/
  │______/________________ Strain
       ↑
   elastic region
```

The exact shape differs significantly among tissues.

The curve provides information about:

* stiffness,
* elastic behavior,
* yield behavior,
* strength,
* and failure.

### Elastic region

In the elastic region, deformation is substantially recoverable when the load is removed.

### Yield point

The **yield point** marks the approximate transition beyond which significant permanent deformation begins.

### Plastic region

In the **plastic region**, removing the load does not return the material completely to its original dimensions.

### Failure

With continued loading, the tissue may eventually reach a point at which structural failure occurs.

---

## 8. Strength

**Strength** is the ability of a material or tissue to withstand mechanical stress without failure.

Depending on the loading condition, one may refer to:

* tensile strength,
* compressive strength,
* or shear strength.

For biological tissues, strength is particularly important because failure can lead to injury.

For example:

* Excessive tensile loading can damage a tendon.
* Excessive compressive loading can damage bone or cartilage.
* Excessive shear can damage tissue structures.

---

## 9. Toughness

**Toughness** is the ability of a material to absorb mechanical energy before failure.

Graphically, toughness is related to the **area under the stress–strain curve up to failure**.

```text
Stress
  │
  │        /\
  │       /  \
  │      /    \
  │_____/______\________ Strain
       ↑
   area under curve
   ≈ toughness
```

A tough tissue can absorb considerable energy before failing.

This is particularly important in biological structures that experience impacts or repeated mechanical loading.

### Strength vs toughness

* **Strength:** how much stress can be tolerated before failure.
* **Toughness:** how much energy can be absorbed before failure.

These should not be confused in an examination answer.

---

## 10. Plasticity

**Plasticity** is the ability of a material to undergo permanent deformation after the applied stress exceeds its elastic limit.

If a tissue is loaded beyond its elastic range, it may not return completely to its original shape.

Thus:

$$
\text{Elastic deformation} \rightarrow \text{recoverable}
$$

$$
\text{Plastic deformation} \rightarrow \text{permanent}
$$

The distinction is important because excessive permanent deformation may compromise the normal function of a biological structure.

---

## 11. Viscoelasticity

One of the most important characteristics of biological tissues is **viscoelasticity**.

**Viscoelasticity** is mechanical behavior that combines characteristics of both an ideal elastic solid and a viscous fluid.

In simple terms, a viscoelastic tissue does not respond only according to the magnitude of the applied force. Its response also depends on **time**.

Many soft biological tissues, including tendons, ligaments, skin, and cartilage, exhibit viscoelastic behavior.

Two important manifestations are **creep** and **stress relaxation**.

### 11.1 Creep

**Creep** is the gradual increase in deformation when a constant load or stress is maintained over time.

For example, if a constant tensile load is applied to a soft tissue:

```text
Constant stress
      │
      v
Tissue deformation
      │
      ├── initially small
      ├── increases with time
      └── eventually approaches a higher value
```

Therefore:

$$
\text{constant stress} \rightarrow \text{increasing strain with time}
$$

### 11.2 Stress relaxation

**Stress relaxation** is the gradual decrease in stress when a tissue is held at a constant deformation.

Thus:

$$
\text{constant strain} \rightarrow \text{decreasing stress with time}
$$

Creep and stress relaxation demonstrate why the mechanical behavior of biological tissues cannot always be described using simple elastic equations.

---

## 12. Anisotropy

**Anisotropy** means that the mechanical properties of a material depend on the direction in which it is loaded.

Biological tissues often have organized internal structures, so their properties can vary with direction.

For example, many fibrous tissues are stronger along the direction of their major collagen fibers than across them.

This is different from an **isotropic** material, whose mechanical properties are approximately the same in all directions.

### Comparison

| Property | Isotropic material | Anisotropic material |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanical behavior | Similar in different directions | Depends on direction |
| Structural organization | More uniform | Directionally organized |
| Biological example | Approximation for some tissues/materials | Tendon and many fibrous tissues |

Anisotropy is important when designing artificial materials intended to replace or interact with tissues having directional mechanical properties.

---

## 13. Other important characteristics of biological tissues

Biological tissues have several additional features that make them different from many conventional engineering materials.

### 13.1 Heterogeneity

A **heterogeneous** material has properties that vary from one location to another.

Biological tissues are often heterogeneous because their composition and structure vary spatially.

For example, bone contains regions with different densities and architectures.

### 13.2 Nonlinear behavior

Many biological tissues do not follow a simple straight-line stress–strain relationship.

Their stiffness can change as the amount of deformation increases.

For example, some soft tissues initially deform relatively easily and then become progressively stiffer as the tissue is stretched.

Therefore, treating every biological tissue as a simple linear elastic material can lead to inaccurate predictions.

### 13.3 Rate dependence

The mechanical response of some tissues depends on **how quickly the tissue is loaded**.

A tissue may respond differently under:

* slow loading,
* rapid loading,
* and repeated loading.

This behavior is particularly relevant to tissues subjected to impacts or dynamic activities.

### 13.4 Remodeling

Living tissues can change their structure in response to their mechanical environment.

This is called **mechanical adaptation** or **remodeling**.

Bone is a classic example. Its structure can adapt over time to changes in mechanical loading.

This means that biological tissues are not simply passive engineering materials; they are **living, adaptive systems**.

## 14. Major biological tissues and their mechanical behavior

The mechanical properties of tissues are strongly related to their **composition and biological function**.

### 14.1 Bone

Bone is a relatively strong and stiff biological material. It provides:

* structural support,
* protection of internal organs,
* attachment points for muscles,
* and a framework for movement.

Bone is a **composite material**, consisting primarily of a mineral phase associated with an organic matrix.

The mineral component contributes substantially to stiffness and compressive strength, while the organic component contributes to toughness and resistance to fracture.

Bone also exhibits anisotropic behavior because its internal structure is organized.

#### Engineering significance

Knowledge of bone mechanics is essential for:

* fracture fixation,
* orthopedic implants,
* artificial joints,
* bone replacement,
* and prosthetic design.

### 14.2 Cartilage

**Cartilage** is a specialized connective tissue found in locations such as joints.

Articular cartilage covers the surfaces of bones within synovial joints and helps provide:

* load distribution,
* shock absorption,
* and low-friction movement.

Its mechanical behavior is strongly influenced by its extracellular matrix and high water content.

Cartilage exhibits significant **viscoelastic and time-dependent behavior**.

#### Engineering significance

Understanding cartilage mechanics is important in:

* artificial joint design,
* cartilage repair,
* tissue engineering,
* and treatment of degenerative joint disorders.

### 14.3 Tendons

A **tendon** connects muscle to bone and transmits muscular force to the skeleton.

Tendons are rich in organized collagen fibers and are therefore particularly suited to **tensile loading**.

Their directional organization gives them strongly anisotropic mechanical behavior.

#### Functional relationship

```text
Muscle contraction
       ↓
     Tendon
       ↓
Force transmitted to bone
       ↓
Joint movement
```

Thus, tendon mechanics are directly related to human movement.

### 14.4 Ligaments

A **ligament** connects bone to bone and contributes to joint stability.

Like tendons, ligaments contain substantial amounts of collagen and are capable of carrying tensile loads.

However, their mechanical and functional roles differ:

* **Tendon:** primarily transmits muscle force.
* **Ligament:** primarily stabilizes and guides a joint.

Understanding ligament mechanics is important in injury treatment, reconstruction, and artificial ligament development.

### 14.5 Skin

Skin is a flexible soft tissue that protects the body and accommodates movement.

Its mechanical behavior is:

* nonlinear,
* viscoelastic,
* and direction-dependent to some extent.

Skin can stretch considerably without immediate failure, but its mechanical behavior changes with factors such as age and structural composition.

Biomechanical knowledge of skin is useful in:

* wound healing,
* artificial skin,
* prosthetic interfaces,
* reconstructive surgery,
* and wearable biomedical devices.

### 14.6 Muscle

Muscle is a mechanically active tissue because it can **generate force through contraction**.

This distinguishes muscle from tissues such as bone, which primarily respond passively to externally applied forces.

Muscle mechanics are important for understanding:

* movement,
* force production,
* joint loading,
* rehabilitation,
* and assistive devices.

---

## 15. Relationship between tissue composition, structure, and mechanical properties

A central principle in biomechanics is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Composition} \rightarrow \text{Structure} \rightarrow \text{Mechanical behavior} \rightarrow \text{Biological function}}
$$

The relationship is not simply one-way, because living tissues can also remodel in response to their mechanical environment.

For example:

**Collagen organization** affects the directional strength of a tissue.

**Mineral content and microstructure** contribute strongly to the stiffness and strength of bone.

**Water and extracellular matrix organization** influence the time-dependent behavior of cartilage and other soft tissues.

Therefore, to understand why a tissue behaves mechanically in a particular way, it is necessary to consider both its **material composition** and its **structural organization**.

---

## 16. Why tissue mechanics matter in bioengineering

The mechanical properties of tissues form the foundation for several bioengineering applications.

When engineers design a device that interacts with the human body, they must consider questions such as:

* Will the device withstand the forces generated during normal activity?
* Will it deform excessively?
* Will it transfer loads appropriately to surrounding tissues?
* Will repeated loading cause fatigue or failure?
* Is its stiffness compatible with the surrounding tissue?
* Will it produce abnormal stresses at the tissue–device interface?

These questions connect biomechanics directly to **prostheses, rehabilitation engineering, biomaterials, and tissue engineering**.

For example, an artificial joint is not designed merely to be strong. Its geometry, stiffness, friction, wear behavior, and interaction with surrounding bone and soft tissue must all be considered.

## 17. Important distinctions

These terms are frequently confused and should be clearly distinguished.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Stress** | Internal force per unit area |
| **Strain** | Relative deformation produced by loading |
| **Stiffness** | Resistance to deformation |
| **Strength** | Ability to withstand stress without failure |
| **Elasticity** | Ability to recover deformation after unloading |
| **Plasticity** | Permanent deformation after exceeding the elastic range |
| **Toughness** | Ability to absorb energy before failure |
| **Viscoelasticity** | Time-dependent behavior combining elastic and viscous characteristics |
| **Anisotropy** | Mechanical properties depend on direction |
| **Heterogeneity** | Properties vary from one location to another |

You should avoid treating these terms as synonyms.

---

## 18. Overall conceptual picture

The mechanical behavior of a biological tissue can be understood as a chain:

```text
External load
     ↓
Mechanical stress develops
     ↓
Tissue deforms → strain
     ↓
Material responds according to its
composition + structure
     ↓
Elastic / plastic / viscoelastic behavior
     ↓
Recovery, permanent deformation, or failure
```

However, because tissues are living systems, the process can extend over longer periods:

```text
Mechanical loading
       ↓
Cellular/mechanical response
       ↓
Adaptation or remodeling
       ↓
Change in tissue structure
       ↓
Change in mechanical properties
```

This second relationship is particularly important in biomechanics because biological tissues can **adapt to their mechanical environment**.
